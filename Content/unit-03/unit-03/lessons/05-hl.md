## concept

The **Hypotenuse-Leg (HL) Theorem** is a special congruence shortcut that applies *exclusively* to right triangles. It states: *If the hypotenuse and one leg of one right triangle are congruent to the hypotenuse and one leg of another right triangle, then the triangles are congruent.* The right angle is a prerequisite — you must confirm it first before HL can be applied.

In any right triangle, the sides have specific roles. The two shorter sides meeting at the right angle are called **legs**, and the longest side, which lies opposite the right angle, is the **hypotenuse**. In △ABC with ∠B = 90°, the legs are AB and BC, while AC (opposite ∠B) is the hypotenuse. Correct identification of the hypotenuse is essential for applying HL — students sometimes confuse the hypotenuse with a leg, especially when a triangle is drawn in an unusual orientation.

Why does HL guarantee congruence even though it looks like only two measurements? The answer is the **Pythagorean Theorem**: in a right triangle, a² + b² = c², where c is the hypotenuse. If you know the hypotenuse (c) and one leg (say a), the other leg is completely determined: b = √(c² − a²). So HL secretly gives you all three sides — making it equivalent to SSS for right triangles. You are not skipping anything; the right angle and the Pythagorean theorem fill in the missing leg automatically.

HL is sometimes listed as a postulate and sometimes as a theorem, depending on the textbook. When treated as a theorem, the proof runs: use the Pythagorean theorem to find the missing leg in each triangle, establish that it is the same (since both triangles have the same hypotenuse and leg), then apply SSS. Either way, the result is the same: right angle + hypotenuse + one leg → congruent triangles.

A critical diagnostic question: *Is this triangle a right triangle?* Before writing a HL justification, your proof must state (with a reason) that ∠B = 90° and ∠E = 90°. Common reasons include "given," "definition of perpendicular lines," or "definition of altitude." Without this setup, HL cannot be invoked. This disciplined habit — checking prerequisites before applying a theorem — is fundamental to rigorous proof-writing.

## reflection

HL is beautifully economical: it exploits a special structural property of right triangles (the Pythagorean relationship) to reduce the number of conditions needed. This is a recurring theme in mathematics — whenever objects have special structure, you can often prove more with less. Right triangles appear everywhere in geometry, trigonometry, and physics, so having a dedicated congruence theorem for them is genuinely useful, not just a curiosity.

HL connects directly to the Pythagorean theorem you studied in earlier grades. Every time you apply HL, you are implicitly using the Pythagorean theorem to fill in the missing leg. This makes HL a natural bridge between the arithmetic of the theorem (computing sides) and the logical framework of congruence proofs. Later in the course, when you study circles, the relationship between a radius, a tangent, and a chord creates right triangles where HL appears naturally.

Coming up next is CPCTC — Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles are Congruent. HL (and all the other congruence tools) are used to *establish* that two triangles are congruent; CPCTC then lets you *extract* information about individual parts. This two-step structure — prove congruence, then invoke CPCTC — is the backbone of most triangle proofs you will write in Lesson 7.
