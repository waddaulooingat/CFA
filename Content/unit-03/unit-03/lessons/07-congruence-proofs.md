## concept

A **triangle congruence proof** is a logical argument — typically written in two-column format — that establishes whether two triangles are congruent and draws conclusions about their individual parts. The two-column format places **Statements** in the left column and **Reasons** in the right column. Every statement must be supported by a reason: "Given," a definition, a postulate, or a previously proven theorem. Nothing can appear in the Statements column without a justification on the right.

The most common structure for a triangle congruence proof follows a three-phase pattern. **Phase 1 (Setup):** Extract given information and use properties (like the Reflexive Property for shared sides, or alternate interior angle theorems for parallel lines) to establish the three conditions needed for a congruence postulate. **Phase 2 (Congruence):** State the triangle congruence with the correct correspondence, citing SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, or HL as the reason. **Phase 3 (Conclusion):** Use CPCTC to extract the specific pair of parts the proof needs, then (if needed) cite a definition (e.g., definition of angle bisector, definition of midpoint) to state the final geometric conclusion.

Valid reasons in a two-column proof include: **Given** (stated in the problem), **Definition of midpoint/bisector/perpendicular**, **Segment Addition Postulate**, **Angle Addition Postulate**, **Reflexive Property of Congruence**, **Symmetric Property**, **Transitive Property**, **Vertical Angles Theorem**, **Alternate Interior Angles Theorem** (when lines are parallel), **Corresponding Angles Theorem**, **Triangle Angle Sum Theorem**, and the five congruence postulates/theorems: **SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, HL**. After the congruence is proven, **CPCTC** is a valid reason.

Planning a proof before writing it is essential. Begin by identifying the "target" — what do you need to prove? Work backwards: if the target is "AB ≅ CD," you need two congruent triangles where AB and CD are corresponding sides, so the question becomes "which triangles contain AB and CD?" Once the triangles are identified, survey the given information and ask which congruence postulate the given information supports. This "backward-forward" planning often reveals the proof path quickly.

A particularly important proof template involves a **shared side**: two triangles that share a segment automatically have one pair of congruent sides (Reflexive Property). This reduces the work to establishing two more conditions — often via given information, parallel lines (for alternate interior angles), or bisectors. Another key template uses **midpoints** and **bisectors**, which directly produce congruent segment or angle pairs as their defining property.

## reflection

Two-column proofs synthesize everything from Unit 3 — and from earlier units. Every reason you write draws on a fact you have already learned: parallel lines from Unit 2, angle relationships from Unit 1, and the congruence postulates from this unit. A triangle congruence proof is a showcase of mathematical connections, not an isolated exercise.

The discipline of proof-writing — demanding a reason for every claim — is one of geometry's most important contributions to logical thinking. This standard of rigor appears in mathematics, computer science (where proofs of algorithm correctness follow similar structures), law (where every legal claim needs a cited precedent), and science (where conclusions must be justified by evidence). Learning to write clear, reason-supported arguments is a transferable skill.

As you move beyond Unit 3, triangle congruence proofs will be the engine powering results about quadrilaterals, circles, and transformations. To prove that a quadrilateral is a parallelogram, you draw a diagonal and prove the two triangles congruent — then CPCTC gives you the parallel sides. To prove a point is equidistant from two sides of an angle, you use a right-triangle pair and HL or AAS. The proof structures you are building now will carry you through the rest of the course and beyond.
