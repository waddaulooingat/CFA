# Skill 1 — Points, Lines, and Planes

**Skill ID:** `geo-u1-point-line-plane`
**Difficulty band:** 1
**Prereqs:** none

---

## Concept

Geometry is built on three undefined terms. They're called "undefined" because we don't try to define them — instead, we describe what they do.

A **point** marks a location. It has no size, no width, no thickness. We draw it as a dot but the dot is just a marker; the point itself is the exact location. We name points with capital letters: *A*, *B*, *C*.

A **line** is a straight path of points that extends forever in both directions. It has length but no width. We name a line two ways: by any two points on it, like $\overleftrightarrow{AB}$, or by a single lowercase script letter, like line $\ell$.

A **plane** is a flat surface that extends forever in every direction. It has length and width but no thickness — like an infinitely large sheet of paper. We name a plane with a capital script letter, like plane $\mathcal{P}$, or by three points on it that aren't on the same line, like plane *ABC*.

Some words we'll use constantly:

- **Collinear** — points lying on the same line. Any two points are automatically collinear (one line through them); three or more points are collinear only if one line passes through all of them.
- **Coplanar** — points or lines lying in the same plane. Any three points are automatically coplanar; four or more require the plane to exist.

A few facts that we'll accept as postulates (statements we take as true without proof):

1. Through any two points there is exactly one line.
2. Through any three non-collinear points there is exactly one plane.
3. If two points lie in a plane, then the entire line containing those points lies in that plane.
4. If two distinct planes intersect, they intersect in exactly one line.

## Worked Example

> Points *D*, *E*, *F* are shown. Line $\overleftrightarrow{DE}$ passes through both *D* and *E*. Is point *F* on line $\overleftrightarrow{DE}$?

**Step 1.** Identify the line. $\overleftrightarrow{DE}$ is the line through *D* and *E*; the arrows in the notation mean it extends forever in both directions.

**Step 2.** Check whether *F* lies on that infinite line — not just between *D* and *E*. Even if *F* is far past *E*, it counts as long as it's on the same straight path.

**Step 3.** Look at the diagram. If *D*, *E*, *F* sit on the same straight path, *F* is on $\overleftrightarrow{DE}$ and the three points are collinear. If *F* is off the path, even slightly, it isn't.

**Answer:** In our diagram, *F* lies off the line, so *F* is *not* on $\overleftrightarrow{DE}$ and the three points are *not* collinear.

## Reflection

Three undefined terms (point, line, plane), two vocabulary words (collinear, coplanar), four postulates. That's the whole skill.

The notation matters because it carries meaning: $\overleftrightarrow{AB}$ is a line (both arrows, infinite both ways), and you'll meet $\overline{AB}$ (segment) and $\overrightarrow{AB}$ (ray) next. Get the arrows right and half the work of a proof is already done.
